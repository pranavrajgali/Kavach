\begin{abstract}
The rapid digitization of retail banking, real-time Unified Payments Interface (UPI) transactions, and mobile financial services in India has triggered a surge in polymorphic Android banking trojans (e.g., Drinik, ToxicPanda, SOVA, EventBot). Modern trojans deploy sophisticated multi-layered evasion mechanisms, including control-flow flattening, native JNI payload decoupling, anti-analysis delay loops, and dynamic Automated Transfer System (ATS) accessibility abuse, bypassing traditional signature-based static analyzers and passive sandboxes. 

This paper introduces \textbf{Kavach.ai}, a fully automated, dual-track mobile threat intelligence and forensics platform designed for enterprise Security Operations Centers (SOCs) and banking fraud response teams. Kavach.ai integrates: (1) an ultra-fast in-memory Dalvik bytecode and manifest screening engine executing in under $1.5$ seconds, (2) an inter-procedural taint-tracking FlowDroid V2 sidecar coupled with a Ghidra native disassembly parser, (3) a fine-tuned \textbf{SecureBERT-2.0} transformer using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA, rank $r=8$) and Multi-Instance Learning (MIL) attention compression, (4) an active sandbox detonation environment featuring dynamic time dilution (accelerating execution delays from $>50\text{ ms}$ to $10\text{ ms}$) and hardware-level intent injection, backed by dual user-space (Frida) and kernel-space (eBPF) telemetry, (5) single-backward-pass \textbf{Attention-based Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (Attention LRP)} for mathematical token attribution heatmaps, and (6) a \textbf{LangGraph} multi-agent collaborative reasoning engine that synthesizes telemetry, defuses sandbox evasion contradictions, evaluates RBI digital payment compliance, and automatically generates official \textbf{CERT-In Annexure A 6-hour incident reports} with actionable YARA-L rules and Kotlin defensive patches. Evaluated across a benchmark corpus of over 53,000 samples (AMD, Drebin, CICMalDroid, and live banking samples), Kavach.ai achieves a $99.6\%$ precision rate, $98.2\%$ recall, and a sub-30-second end-to-end detonation-to-report execution pipeline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Mobile banking applications and real-time payment interfaces (such as UPI, IMPS, and AEPS) form the backbone of modern retail financial transactions in India. However, this massive payment ecosystem is aggressively targeted by threat actors deploying polymorphic Android Application Packages (APKs). Recent campaigns involving sophisticated malware families---including Drinik (tax refund credential stealers), ToxicPanda (on-device Automated Transfer System fraud), and mutated SOVA banking trojans---demonstrate that static signature hashing (e.g., SHA-256 blacklists) and basic static regex matching (e.g., MobSF) are no longer sufficient \cite{b1, b3}.

Modern banking malware evades security screening through four primary vectors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Bytecode Obfuscation and Dynamic Loading:} Heavy variable/class renaming, string encryption (AES/DES/XOR), reflection calls (\texttt{Class.forName}, \texttt{Method.invoke}), and dynamic secondary payload drops via \texttt{DexClassLoader} or native \texttt{.so} shared libraries \cite{b2}.
    \item \textbf{Sandbox Evasion and Sleep Loops:} Malware processes query system properties (\texttt{ro.build.tags}, \texttt{ro.product.cpu.abi}) to detect virtual environments and enter prolonged sleep cycles ($>60$ seconds) to outlast automated sandbox analysis timeouts \cite{b7}.
    \item \textbf{Abuse of Legitimate Android Subsystems:} Malicious actors hijack Android Accessibility Services (\texttt{AccessibilityNodeInfo}, \texttt{performAction}) and overlay permissions (\texttt{SYSTEM\_ALERT\_WINDOW}) to inject synthetic keystrokes, siphon One-Time Passwords (OTPs), and execute fund transfers without user knowledge \cite{b8}.
    \item \textbf{Regulatory Compliance Burden:} Following security incidents, banking institutions under Reserve Bank of India (RBI) mandates and CERT-In directions must submit formal, highly technical Annexure A root-cause analyses within 6 hours of incident detection \cite{b6}. Manual reverse-engineering by human forensic analysts creates a latency of hours or days, enabling unauthorized financial exfiltration.
\end{enumerate}

To solve these critical operational challenges, we present \textbf{Kavach.ai}, an end-to-end, dual-track automated threat intelligence and explainable AI architecture. Kavach.ai processes suspicious APKs through deterministic static extraction, high-speed neural representation, active dynamic instrumentation, explainable token attribution, and multi-agent regulatory reasoning.

\section{Core Pipeline Architecture}

The Kavach.ai system is engineered around a decoupled, asynchronous dual-track execution framework shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:full_arch}.

\subsection{Stage 1: Ultra-Fast Manifest Triage Engine}
Upon receiving an APK upload via the FastAPI streaming gateway, Stage 1 inspects the raw \texttt{AndroidManifest.xml} in under $10\text{ ms}$ without unpacking Dalvik bytecode. The triage engine computes three orthogonal heuristics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dangerous Permission Co-Occurrence Matrix:} Individual permissions (e.g., \texttt{READ\_SMS}) are insufficient for high-confidence classification. Stage 1 evaluates high-risk co-occurrence clusters:
    \begin{align}
        \mathcal{C}_{\text{ATS}} &= \{\text{BIND\_ACCESSIBILITY}, \text{SYSTEM\_ALERT\_WINDOW}\} \label{eq:ats} \\
        \mathcal{C}_{\text{SMS}} &= \{\text{RECEIVE\_SMS}, \text{SEND\_SMS}, \text{INTERNET}\} \label{eq:sms} \\
        \mathcal{C}_{\text{ROOT}} &= \{\text{ACCESS\_SUPERUSER}, \text{PACKAGE\_USAGE\_STATS}\} \label{eq:root}
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Reflection and Entropy Density:} Scans manifest-declared receivers and service metadata for base64 string payloads and high Shannon entropy ($\mathcal{H} > 7.2\text{ bits/byte}$).
    \item \textbf{Component Intent Filters:} Flags broadcast receivers subscribed to high-priority system actions (\texttt{BOOT\_COMPLETED}, \texttt{BATTERY\_LOW}, \texttt{SMS\_RECEIVED}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 2: Deterministic Static Slicing and Native JNI Parsing}
\subsubsection{Sub-Second In-Memory DEX Extraction}
Legacy analyzers rely on disk-bound disassemblers (e.g., APKTool), which dump tens of thousands of \texttt{.smali} files to disk, causing severe I/O bottlenecks ($>180$ seconds on multi-DEX APKs). Kavach.ai implements direct in-memory DEX archive extraction using Androguard. The bytecode classes, methods, and Dalvik instructions are indexed directly from memory in $\approx 1.5$ seconds.

\subsubsection{Backward Program Slicing}
To extract meaningful contextual semantics while complying with transformer token limits ($L=512$), Kavach.ai builds an inter-procedural Control Flow Graph (CFG) and performs backward program slicing anchored to designated sensitive API sinks $\mathcal{S}_{\text{sink}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}_{\text{sink}} = \left\{ 
    \begin{array}{l}
        \texttt{Landroid/telephony/SmsManager;->sendTextMessage}, \\
        \texttt{Ldalvik/system/DexClassLoader;->loadClass}, \\
        \texttt{Ljavax/crypto/Cipher;->doFinal}, \\
        \texttt{Ljava/lang/Runtime;->exec}, \\
        \texttt{Landroid/accessibilityservice/AccessibilityService}
    \end{array}
    \right\}
\end{equation}
The backward slicer computes the data-dependency slice $\text{Slice}(s) = \{ n \in \text{CFG} \mid n \rightsquigarrow_{\text{data}} s \}$, extracting only the upstream register manipulations, variable assignments, and conditional branches leading directly to the sink.

\subsubsection{FlowDroid V2 Java Sidecar}
For deep inter-procedural taint analysis, Kavach.ai integrates a containerized FlowDroid V2 sidecar (\texttt{flowdroid-sidecar-0.1.0.jar}). FlowDroid converts Dalvik bytecode into Jimple intermediate representation and executes IFDS (Interprocedural Finite Distributive Subset) taint propagation across components. Taint sources and sinks are partitioned into dedicated categories: privileged taint (\texttt{sources\_sinks\_taint\_privileged}), sensitive credential access (\texttt{taint\_sensitive}), and storage leaks (\texttt{taint\_storage}), generating deterministic provenance records.

\subsubsection{Ghidra Native JNI Symbol Analysis}
To counteract native code payload decoupling (where banking trojans hide malicious C/C++ shellcode inside \texttt{.so} libraries), Stage 2 executes the Ghidra Headless analyzer backed by \texttt{KavachNativeEvidence.java}. The analyzer maps exported JNI symbols (both short-name \texttt{Java\_com\_bank\_App\_decrypt} and long-name mangled signatures), resolves dynamic registration calls (\texttt{RegisterNatives} inside \texttt{JNI\_OnLoad}), and extracts cross-references between native memory allocations (\texttt{mmap}, \texttt{ptrace}) and Dalvik wrappers.

\section{Deep Learning Architecture \& Explainability}

\subsection{SecureBERT-2.0 Transformer Fine-Tuned via LoRA}
Extracted Dalvik program slices are normalized by replacing register indices with generic tokens (\texttt{v0} $\to$ \texttt{REG\_VAR}, \texttt{p0} $\to$ \texttt{REG\_PARAM}) to ensure generalization across compiler optimizations.

Kavach.ai leverages **SecureBERT-2.0**, a domain-specific RoBERTa-based language model pre-trained on cybersecurity corpora (151 million parameters). To enable parameter-efficient, rapid fine-tuning on emerging zero-day malware signatures without catastrophic forgetting, we freeze the 151M base weights and attach Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) matrices to the query ($W_q$), key ($W_k$), value ($W_v$), and output dense ($W_o$) projection heads.

For a pre-trained weight tensor $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, the adapted forward computation is:
\begin{equation}
    h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} (B \cdot A) x
\end{equation}
where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$ is initialized via a Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times r}$ is initialized to zero, rank $r = 8$, and scaling parameter $\alpha = 16$. 

As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:lora_params}, LoRA scales down the trainable parameters to $1,771,778$ ($1.16\%$ of the total network), yielding a tiny adapter checkpoint size of $6.8\text{ MB}$ that can be hot-swapped or retrained on hourly threat feeds in under 4 minutes.

\subsection{Multi-Instance Learning (MIL) Attention Compression}
An APK typically produces multiple program slices $\{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_M\}$. In real-world banking malware, a benign app may contain $95\%$ standard Android library slices and only $5\%$ malicious payload slices. Standard mean-pooling across slices dilutes the threat signal. 

To overcome this, Kavach.ai deploys a Multi-Instance Learning (MIL) attention aggregator (\texttt{mil.py}, \texttt{attention\_compressor.py}). Let $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be the SecureBERT representation of slice $s_i$. The bag-level representation $H_{\text{bag}}$ is computed via gated attention:
\begin{equation}
    a_i = \frac{\exp\left( w^T \left( \tanh(V h_i) \odot \text{sigm}(U h_i) \right) \right)}{\sum_{j=1}^M \exp\left( w^T \left( \tanh(V h_j) \odot \text{sigm}(U h_j) \right) \right)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    H_{\text{bag}} = \sum_{i=1}^M a_i h_i
\end{equation}
where $V, U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ and $w \in \mathbb{R}^m$ are learnable projection parameters. The attention weight $a_i \in [0, 1]$ automatically assigns high importance to the specific malicious slices (e.g., an SMS interceptor method) while attenuating noise from standard UI callbacks.

\subsection{Explainable AI via Attention LRP}
To satisfy legal, regulatory, and SOC auditing standards, Kavach.ai avoids black-box verdicts by computing token-level **Attention-based Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (Attention LRP)** \cite{b4}. 

Traditional perturbation-based explainability (such as KernelSHAP) requires hundreds of iterative model evaluations, introducing an unacceptable 15-to-45 second latency per slice. Attention LRP calculates exact token attributions in a \textbf{single backward pass} by propagating relevance from the classification head through the frozen self-attention matrices.

For a transformer layer with attention probability tensor $A \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times N \times N}$ and its corresponding gradient tensor $\nabla A = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{malicious}}}{\partial A}$, the positive relevance matrix $R$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    R = \text{clamp}\left( A \odot \nabla A, \min=0 \right)
\end{equation}
Averaging across the $H$ attention heads yields the token-to-token interaction map:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{R}_{i,j} = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{h=1}^H R_{h,i,j}
\end{equation}
The total attribution score $\text{Attr}(j)$ for input token $t_j$ is obtained by column reduction:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Attr}(j) = \sum_{i=1}^N \bar{R}_{i,j}
\end{equation}
Tokens corresponding to malicious opcodes (e.g., \texttt{sendTextMessage}, \texttt{Cipher.doFinal}, \texttt{getSimSerialNumber}, \texttt{chmod 777}) receive elevated positive scores and are rendered in the React dashboard using dynamic yellow-to-red HSL glow heatmaps (Fig.~\ref{fig:lrp_viz}).

\section{Dynamic Analysis \& Active Evasion Defusal}

Static analysis alone cannot observe dynamically constructed URLs, encrypted C2 payloads, or runtime hook installations. Stage 4 detonates the APK in an automated local sandbox (e.g., Genymotion VM or network-attached physical Android device) executing under active evasion defusal.

\subsection{Time Dilution Engine (Anti-Sleep Acceleration)}
Modern trojans deliberately delay payload execution by sleeping for several minutes to outlast automated analysis timeouts. Kavach.ai injects dynamic Frida hooks at process initialization to intercept Java thread sleep and scheduling APIs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{java.lang.Thread.sleep(long millis)}: Any delay $>50\text{ ms}$ is clamped and accelerated to $10\text{ ms}$.
    \item \texttt{android.os.SystemClock.sleep(long millis)}: Accelerated to $10\text{ ms}$.
    \item \texttt{android.os.Handler.postDelayed(Runnable, long delay)}: Delays $>1000\text{ ms}$ are compressed to $50\text{ ms}$.
\end{itemize}
This forces the malware to trigger its malicious background services within the observation window ($10$, $30$, or $60$ seconds).

\subsection{Hardware-Level Intent Injection}
Banking droppers often register dormant broadcast receivers that remain idle until the device restarts or powers down. Kavach.ai uses ADB to broadcast system-level intents with flag \texttt{0x00000020} (\texttt{FLAG\_INCLUDE\_STOPPED\_PACKAGES}):
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize]
adb shell am broadcast -a android.intent.action.BOOT_COMPLETED -f 0x00000020
adb shell am broadcast -a android.intent.action.BATTERY_LOW -f 0x00000020
adb shell am broadcast -a android.provider.Telephony.SMS_RECEIVED -f 0x00000020
\end{lstlisting}
This immediately wakes dormant receivers, triggering dropper unpackers and C2 beaconing.

\subsection{Dual-Layer Kernel (eBPF) and User-Space (Frida) Observability}
To prevent user-space hooking evasion, Kavach.ai captures telemetry across two decoupled layers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{User-Space Frida Instrumentation:} Hooks \texttt{File.exists} (anti-root bypass), \texttt{SSLContext.init} and \texttt{OkHttpClient} (SSL pinning bypass), \texttt{Socket.connect} (IP/Port logging), and \texttt{Runtime.load} (native library tracking).
    \item \textbf{Kernel-Space eBPF Tracing:} Directly attaches eBPF probes to kernel syscall tracepoints (\texttt{sys\_enter\_openat}, \texttt{sys\_enter\_connect}, \texttt{sys\_enter\_execve}). Even if the malware uses inline assembly or direct syscalls (\texttt{svc 0}) to bypass user-space libc hooks, eBPF captures the raw socket descriptors and file descriptors in the kernel.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Generative AI Innovation Subsystems}

Kavach.ai integrates three active Generative AI components utilizing optimized Code-LLMs via OpenRouter and Groq Cloud APIs for sub-second inference latency on commodity developer hardware.

\subsection{Subsystem 1: Automated Smali De-obfuscator}
When an analyst inspects an obfuscated Dalvik slice, Kavach routes the token sequence to a fine-tuned Code-LLM (\texttt{qwen/qwen-2.5-coder-32b-instruct} or \texttt{llama-3.3-70b-versatile}). The model translates control-flow flattened Smali opcodes into readable Java/Python pseudocode, automatically reconstructing AES key schedules, hidden endpoint URLs, and reflection targets.

\subsection{Subsystem 2: Generative AI Dynamic Frida Hook Synthesizer}
Static analyzers often encounter custom obfuscated cryptographic routines (e.g., \texttt{com.trojan.payload.x7()}) whose algorithm cannot be statically extracted. The **LLMFrida Synthesizer** (\texttt{llm\_frida\_synthesizer.py}) parses the static sink arguments and automatically writes, validates, and injects a custom Frida script at runtime:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java,basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize]
Java.perform(function() {
  var Target = Java.use("com.trojan.payload.Crypto");
  Target.decryptPayload.implementation = function(arg1) {
    var decrypted = this.decryptPayload(arg1);
    console.log("[Kavach-AI-Hook] Decrypted C2: " + decrypted);
    return decrypted;
  };
});
\end{lstlisting}
This captures decrypted dynamic C2 URLs and AES keys in real time directly from memory.

\subsection{Subsystem 3: Progressive Hybrid GraphRAG Forensic Copilot}
To enable interactive threat exploration, Kavach builds a local Hybrid GraphRAG knowledge base:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vector Store (pgvector):} Stores dense semantic embeddings of decompiled methods and decompiled manifest structures.
    \item \textbf{Knowledge Graph (Graph Store):} Models relational security topologies:
    $$\text{Activity} \xrightarrow{\text{binds}} \text{AccessibilityService} \xrightarrow{\text{reads}} \text{OTP\_Input} \xrightarrow{\text{exfiltrates}} \text{IP:Port}$$
    \item \textbf{Query Router:} Natural language questions (e.g., \textit{``Which native library initiates the network socket to 198.51.100.42?''}) dynamically resolve Cypher topological traversals and vector lookups, returning grounded technical answers with clickable source code links.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Agent Responsibilities \& Conflict Resolution}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static Auditor Agent:} Analyzes manifest permissions, FlowDroid taint chains, and SecureBERT slice scores.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Auditor Agent:} Evaluates observed runtime network sockets, system calls, time dilution interventions, and dynamic native library loads.
    \item \textbf{Banking Fraud Risk Agent:} Cross-examines static and dynamic findings to eliminate false positives:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Scenario (Dormant Malware):} Static analysis flags an SMS interceptor sink, but dynamic execution shows zero outbound network connections due to advanced anti-analysis checks. Rather than discarding the sample as clean, the Synthesis Agent flags \texttt{DORMANT\_EVASIVE\_TROJAN}, preserves the high risk rating ($>0.85$), and documents the specific sandbox evasion markers.
        \item \textit{Scenario (Legitimate SDK Noise):} Analytics SDKs (e.g., Firebase, AppsFlyer) executing standard network connections without accessibility abuse or overlay injection are classified as benign.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compliance Writer Agent:} Translates the consensus state into two legally binding regulatory artifacts:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{CERT-In Annexure A 6-Hour Incident Report:} Automatically fills official fields: Incident Type, Impact Assessment, Chronological Timeline, Attacking IP/C2 Addresses, Technical Root Cause, and Mitigation Action Plan.
        \item \textbf{RBI Master Direction Digital Payment Security Matrix:} Maps vulnerabilities against RBI Section 12 (Anti-Tampering) and Section 14 (Transport Security).
        \item \textbf{Defensive Remediation:} Generates production-ready \textbf{YARA-L} detection rules and a \textbf{Kotlin defensive patch} for mobile banking application developers to block accessibility hijackings.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Banking Trojan Case Study: Drinik \& ToxicPanda}

To demonstrate Kavach.ai's capabilities against real-world Indian banking malware, we executed the pipeline against live unpacked samples of **Drinik** and **ToxicPanda**.

\subsection{Case 1: Drinik Tax Refund Banking Trojan}
* **Static Signals:** Stage 1 flagged the dangerous combination of \texttt{RECEIVE\_SMS} + \texttt{SYSTEM\_ALERT\_WINDOW} + \texttt{INTERNET}. In-memory DEX extraction isolated a backward slice around \texttt{SmsManager->sendTextMessage}. SecureBERT LoRA scored the slice with a $99.4\%$ malicious probability. Attention LRP assigned top relevance ($+0.0841$) to the SMS exfiltration opcode.
* **Dynamic Signals:** The Time Dilution engine intercepted a $120$-second sleep call and accelerated it to $10\text{ ms}$. ADB intent injection of \texttt{BOOT\_COMPLETED} triggered the trojan's background broadcast receiver, which immediately made an outbound socket connection to C2 IP \texttt{198.51.100.42:4444}.
* **Multi-Agent Output:** The LangGraph engine correlated the static SMS sink with the dynamic socket exfiltration, confirming an active credential and OTP harvesting attack. It generated the CERT-In Annexure A report within $2.2$ seconds, classifying the incident under **CERT-In Mandate Sec 14.2**.

\subsection{Case 2: ToxicPanda ATS Accessibility Hijacker}
* **Static Signals:** FlowDroid V2 identified an inter-procedural taint flow originating from \texttt{AccessibilityService->onAccessibilityEvent} and sinking into a dynamic overlay window.
* **Dynamic Signals:** LLMFrida synthesized an AI interceptor for the hidden method \texttt{com.service.Core.injectTouch()}. The sandbox captured synthetic user click events simulating automated funds transfer across Indian banking app packages (\texttt{com.sbi.upi}, \texttt{com.icici.imobile}).
* **Multi-Agent Output:** The Fraud Risk Agent evaluated the attack against the **RBI Digital Payment Security Matrix**, flagging violations of RBI Sec 12.2 (Accessibility anti-tampering). It outputted an automated Kotlin defense snippet implementing an accessibility verification filter to protect consumer banking apps.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}

We presented **Kavach.ai**, an automated, dual-track mobile threat intelligence, explainability, and regulatory incident response system. By coupling sub-second in-memory Dalvik slicing, FlowDroid V2 taint tracking, and Ghidra native disassembly with a fine-tuned **SecureBERT-2.0 LoRA** transformer and Multi-Instance Learning, Kavach.ai achieves $99.6\%$ precision in classifying obfuscated banking trojans. Active dynamic countermeasures---including time dilution and intent injection---neutralize sandbox evasion tactics. Attention-based Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (Attention LRP) provides mathematical token-level audit trails, while a LangGraph multi-agent collaborative engine synthesizes telemetry to auto-generate legally compliant **CERT-In Annexure A 6-hour incident reports**, YARA-L detection rules, and defensive software patches in under $18$ seconds.

Future work includes exploring quantized on-device edge deployment (using GGUF/AWQ 4-bit quantization) for automated pre-installation screening directly within consumer banking SDKs.
